1  Climate change drove the deep-time assembly of biological diversity

Abstract

Often restricted by the availability of historical data, the study of biodiversity assembly often focuses on contemporary environmental conditions and short-term biodiversity surveys. Newly available paleoclimatic reconstructions combined with georeferenced and dated fossil records offer the opportunity to elucidate how deep-time dynamics have shaped the assembly of biodiversity. Here, using spatially explicit paleo-climatic reconstructions and fossil records for North America and Europe, we evaluate the relative effects of the magnitude of directional climate change, and the instability of climate on the replacement of mammal taxa through time and across trophic levels. Over the last 20 million years in the Neogene, the magnitude and direction of temporal climate change emerged as the dominant drivers of large mammal compositional turnover, with warming driving stronger effects in fossil turnover than cooling. Climatic instability and mean temperature alone have little effect on fossil turnover. Our results show that a 1 °C Ma⁻¹ directional change in temperature reduced taxonomic replacement by approximately 25%, whereas a 1 °C Ma⁻¹ increase in temperature instability reduced replacement by up to7.5% with asymmetric responses between trophic levels. Climatic instability more strongly affected temporal replacement of herbivorous taxa relative to carnivores, but responses were idiosyncratic between continents. Such pattern indicates a tighter coupling of herbivores to historical climate-driven changes in plant community composition, whereas having only indirect effects in higher trophic levels such as carnivores. Taken together, our results suggest that with ongoing rates of climate warming in the order of 0.2–0.3 °C per decade, a severe and unpredictable reorganization of global biodiversity can be expected.

1.1 Introduction

The biological diversity we see today has assembled over millions of years with evolutionary processes unfolding together with global climate change across geological time. The formation of global biogeographic regions, for example, is often attributed to plate tectonics movement and historical climate (Mazel et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the growing availability of spatially and temporally explicit biodiversity and paleoclimatic data offer new opportunities to infer the role of pre-historical climate change in the genesis of global biodiversity (Kocsis et al., 2021; Briega-Alvarez et al., 2025).

Over deep time, the influence of climate change on modern biodiversity can be tracked in repeated episodes of extinction, diversification, and biodiversity reorganization (Albert & Carvalho 2011; Morales-Garcia 2020; Daru et al., 2017). Fossil records in deep time provide a window into those evolutionary episodes (Jackson & Blois 2015; DiMichele et al., 2004; Erwin 2009) and additional insights into processes that extend well beyond the Quaternary (0-2.8Ma) (Blanco et al. 2021; Mottl et al. 2021; Jabot et al. 2020), which has been the primary focus of previous work on the topic. Digitally available fossil datasets with geo-chrono-referenced fossil records (e.g. NOW, PBDB fossil databases) (Vzliobaite et al., 2023) open new opportunities for modelling the assembly of biodiversity across the geological time scale (Jablonski & Shubin 2015; Blanco et al. 2021; Mottl et al. 2021; Jabot et al. 2020).  When integrated with spatiotemporal paleoclimatic reconstructions, fossil records could reveal how planetary climate change shaped biodiversity in the absence anthropogenic stressors (Chang & HilleRisLambers 2016; DiMichele et al., 2004; Lyons & Wagner 2009).

The Neogene (23Ma – 2Ma) was an evolutionary dynamic period in the build-up of global biodiversity. Periods of drastic climatic changes led to the formation of many current temperate biomes (Zachos et al, 2001 ) and coincided with the emergence of many modern plant and animal families (Gunnell et al., 1995; Gibert et al., 2022) as well as the extinction of many others that were previously widespread during the Paleogene (66Ma – 23 Ma) (Costeur et al., 2004). During this period, the Earth’s climate transitioned from a greenhouse to an icehouse state, culminating in the onset of the Northern Hemisphere glaciation around 2-3 million Ma (Kennett et al., 1995). At least two complementary attributes of paleoclimatic dynamics could have influenced the assembly of regional biodiversity during that period. First, the magnitude of directional climatic change between adjacent geological periods, expressed as the velocity of climate change (in C) per unit of time (1Ma) (Zachos et al., 2001; Galvan et al., 2025). Second, the climatic instability, expressed as spatiotemporal dispersion in local climate change rates (Vrba, 1992; Dynesius & Jansson, 2000; Finnegan et al., 2015). Climate change magnitude and climate instability across the Neogene could have jointly shaped the assembly of regional biodiversity, but their relative influence remains unexplored.

Episodes of high directional climate change could have increased rates of mammalian taxa replacement in regional biotas by triggering speciation events and/or increasing extinctions rates.  Climate-driven species factories (Stebbins 1974; Bernor et al., 1996; Eronen et al., 2012) can emerge in regions with a high magnitude of directional climate change, such as increasing aridity or cooling, where preadapted taxa gain a competitive advantage, diversify, and give rise to new lineages suited to the conditions of the near future. For example, during the gradual cooling of Earth during the Neogene (23Ma – 2Ma), ecosystems transition from warm evergreen subtropical woodlands to cooler, more seasonal deciduous forests (Jablonski, 2008; Barnosky et al., 2011; Reddin et al., 2020), many forest-adapted mammal taxa declined or went extinct, while open-habitat specialists such as bovids and equids expanded through adaptive radiations driven by ecological specialization (Jernvall & Fortelius 2002; Raia et al. 2011).  As North America became drier than Eurasia during the middle Miocene–Pliocene, arid-adapted groups such as horses, camels, and canids evolved there first and later invaded the Old World.  In addition, directional shifts in climate change such as the transitions between prolonged warming and cooling phases (or vice versa) can precipitate extinction cascades but also promote adaptive reorganizations (Pinsky et al., 2025; Lewthwaite et al., 2017; Kiessling et al., 2024) depending on how rapidly populations and traits respond to the climatic change shift (Benton & Pearson, 2001; Pimm et al., 1988). Morphological and genetic data show that climate-driven changes in temperature, aridity, and productivity influence body size, tooth morphology, and dispersal, often with temporal lags in the evolutionary response (MacFadden, 2000; Hadly et al., 2004). During rapid environmental change, trait–climate mismatches increase range shifts, extinctions, leading to community reassembly and elevating taxonomic replacement.(Guerin et al., 2013; Fitzpatrick et al., 2013).

In contrast to species factories, which are macroevolutionary sources of diversification (Fortelius et al., 2014), climatically stable regions act as macroevolutionary sinks, preserving past diversity with limited contribution to future radiations (Stebbins 1974; Fortelius et al., 2014; Mirzaie Ataabadi et al., 2013; Vasconcelos et al., 2021). In climatically stable regions, low taxonomic replacement is expected mainly due to reduced extinction rather than increased speciation or immigration (Vasconcelos et al., 2022). Long-term climatic stability promotes lineage persistence within suitable environments, limiting range contraction and local extinction that drive taxonomic replacement. (Fortelius & Zhang, 2006; Casanovas-Vilar et al., 2010; Vasconcelos et al., 2021). For instance, during the Miocene (23Ma-5Ma), the Iberian Peninsula acted as a climatic oasis where suids, bovids, and small carnivores persisted after going extinct elsewhere in Europe (Casanovas-Vilar et al., 2010; Fortelius et al., 2014). Despite faunal exchange with Africa and Central Europe, these lineages in the Iberian peninsula likely maintained low local taxonomic replacement while supplying taxa to more climatically variable regions. (Vasconcelos et al., 2021; Agustí & Antón, 2002).  These dynamics raise the question of whether climatic change and instability affect taxonomic replacement differently across trophic groups, and whether these historical measures better predict replacement than static contemporary climate gradients.

Here, we examine alternative hypotheses regarding the role of climate dynamics in driving the taxonomic replacement of mammalian fossil assemblage during the Neogene (Figure 1). Specifically, A) regions experiencing low levels of directional climatic change (i.e. magnitude) and high levels of instability are expected to show minimal fossil taxonomic replacement (Figure 1a.A, Figure 1b.A) (H1-stability), whereas those experiencing B) a high magnitude of climate change and low instability are expected to show elevated rates of taxonomic replacement. (Figure 1a.B, Figure 1b.B) (H2-directional change). Finally, C) a low magnitude of climate change and high instability may generate episodic pulses of taxonomic replacement without sustained faunal replacement (Figure 1a.C, Figure 1b.C) (H3-Oscillatory climate). D) When both climatic magnitude and instability are high, their effects may compound, producing accelerated and recurrent taxonomic replacement as ecosystems experience rapid and irregular environmental shifts. (Figure 1a.D, Figure 1b.D) (H4- compound instability).

1.2 Methods

Temporal scope

This study covers the Neogene period (23Ma - 2.6Ma). This geological period of Earth was characterized by significant global climatic changes (Kennett et al., 1995) and therefore provides an ideal case study. Broadly, the Neogene saw the emergence of many modern plant and animal families, as well as the extinction of many others that were previously widespread during the Paleogene (66Ma – 23 Ma) (Costeur et al., 2004). During this period, the Earth’s climate transitioned from a greenhouse to an icehouse state, culminating in the onset of the Northern Hemisphere glaciation around 2-3 million Ma (Kennett et al., 1995). During the Neogene, Northern Hemisphere ecosystems experience the transformation of tropical areas in high latitudes into open landscapes such as savannas and prairies (Retallack 1997; 2001), as well as the expansion of temperate and boreal forests (Budantsev 1992). These changes were driven by a combination of tectonic and oceanographic events, including the establishment of the modern-day Gulf Stream Atlantic Ocean current, the uplift of the Rocky Mountains in North America and the Alps in Europe (Retallack 1997; 2001; Kovar 2003; Barash 2011)

Data

Fossil data

For this study, we gathered large-mammal fossil data from the NOW database (Vzliobaite et al., 2023). Fossils of large mammals are particularly useful to uncover macro-evolutionary trends as they can preserve biogeographical patterns with high spatial fidelity (Darroch et al., 2021). Moreover, the results of centuries of paleontological research are now digitally available in aggregated fossil databases with standardized taxonomic nomenclature and geotagged records. Our queried subset of the database contained 18349 species level fossil records spanning six (6) mammal orders: Artiodactyla (40%), Carnivora (26%), Perissodactylia (24%), Primates (1.5%), and Proboscidea (8.6%). We assigned each fossil mammal record to a trophic guild and trophic role based on its taxonomic order. Orders dominated by plant-eating species (Proboscidea, Perissodactyla, and Artiodactyla) were classified as herbivores and primary consumers. Primates were classified as omnivores occupying intermediate trophic roles, and Carnivora were classified as carnivores occupying higher trophic levels. These trophic assignments were added to the fossil dataset by matching each record’s taxonomic order to a reference classification table, while retaining all fossil observations.  We excluded records a) georeferenced outside the North America and Europe continents (3068 records), and b) where the genus was classified as indeterminate (“indet.”) (4215 records). We ended with a total of 11066 species level fossil records (Table S1).To reduce taxonomic bias in sampling completeness we focused subsequent analyses in records aggregated to the genus level, obtaining a total of unique 6843 genus-level fossil space-time observations.

Paleo-atmospheric temperature data

We used published reconstructed near-surface air temperatures for the Cenozoic (60 Ma to present) at 1 Myr temporal resolution (Hagen et al. 2019). Hagen’s paleoclimatic reconstructions integrate paleotopographic models from Scotese’s PaleoAtlas (Scotese & Wright, 2018) with lithological and fossil-based climate proxies, including coal, evaporites, bauxites, tillites, and high-latitude occurrences of palms and mangroves (Boucot et al., 2013). Climatic structure was reconstructed by projecting Köppen climatic zones onto paleogeographic maps at 5-Myr intervals and assigning zone-specific mean annual temperatures adjusted to match Cenozoic global temperature trends (Royer et al., 2004). Temperature lapse rates derived from modern elevation and WorldClim2 data (Fick & Hijmans, 2017) were applied to paleotopographic reconstructions to generate gridded, topography-corrected temperature surfaces through time. Full methods to replicate the paleo atmospheric reconstruction are found in (Hagen et al. 2019).  From Hagen’s paleoclimatic reconstruction dataset, we extracted the subset corresponding to the temporal span of our fossil data (23–2 Ma) and generated a temperature raster stack for North America and Europe.

Using the paleo atmospheric temperature raster stack, we modeled each grid cell’s climate history with a spline-based smoother (Hastie T 1992). The atmospheric temperature stack was converted to a location-by-time matrix in which rows represent spatial grid cells and columns represent time steps.  Then, we built a B-spline basis across the raster layers (time points 23–2 Ma) and fitted it independently per cell, with flexibility set by basis dimension (df = 6) and polynomial degree (degree = 3) (Guillot et al., 2013). Fitted coefficients were estimated via ordinary least-squares for all cells simultaneously. With this procedure, we generated a raster stack with three climatic predictors 1) Mean annual temperature (Ŷ) was the GAM prediction at each cell and time slice (Figure S1). 2) Climate change magnitude (dY) was the first temporal derivative of the smoothed temperature mean (°C Myr⁻¹) (Figure S2). 3) Climatic instability was the standard deviation of mean temperature values within a ±2 Ma moving window around each time step (Figure S3) (Phipps, 2022; Frieler et al., 2025).

Statistical analyses

Establishing fossil paleo-assemblages

To generate fossil paleo-assemblages, we used a hexagonal equal-area grid to assign fossil paleocoordinates to spatial bins at resolution 3 of Uber’s H3 geospatial indexing system, corresponding to an average distance of 103.6 km between adjacent cells. This procedure was implemented using the bin_space() function from the paleoverse R package (v1.4.0) (Figure S4). Temporally, fossil observations were assigned to discrete time bins defined by the midpoint between the minimum and maximum radiocarbon-based age estimates reported in the NOW database, with midpoints rounded using R’s round-to-even convention (Figure S5). Paleo-assemblages were then defined as the set of unique fossil genera occurring within each hexagonal cell and time bin (Figure S4). Assemblages containing fewer than three genera were excluded from subsequent analyses. In total, we generated 559 unique fossil paleoassemblages for all mammal taxa, 489 for herbivores, and 240 for carnivores.

Estimating turnover among fossil paleo assemblages

For each continent, we constructed a genus-paleo assemblage incidence matrix representing the presence (1) or absence (0) of each fossilgenus across all paleo-assemblages in space and time. For these matrices, we calculated turnover between paleo assemblages using the Simpson turnover index via the beta.sim output of the beta.pair() function in the betapart R package (Figure S6). This measure emphasizes genus replacement (i.e. turnover) by separating the degree of shared taxa independent of richness differences (i.e. nestedness), allowing comparable estimates of turnover between Europe and North America despite differences in continental biodiversity. (Baselga et al., 2013). Therefore, making Simpson index a reliable basis for tracking large-scale biogeographical patterns from sparse fossil records.

Linking climate change to fossil paleo assemblage turnover

Compositional turnover was modeled with generalized dissimilarity models (GDMs) (Fitzpatrick et al., 2025; 2021) on pairwise genus-level dissimilarities between fossil assemblages.  We matched fossil assemblages to their local spatiotemporal climatic conditions (Ŷ, dY, instability) by intersecting their coordinates with 1-Myr-binned midpoint ages. Geographic distance (km), temporal separation (Ma), and climatic predictors (Ŷ, dY, instability) were included as nonlinear I-spline gradients in the model (Ferrier & Guisan, 2006; Fitzpatrick et al., 2021; Manion et al., 2017). To control for differences in sampling intensity and richness across fossil assemblages (Figure S7), we used the minimum assemblage richness of fossil assemblage pairs as weights. Models were fitted separately for Europe/North America and all mammals/herbivores/carnivores. We assessed importance of climatic variables on fossil genus turnover using the I-spline heights, with continental/guild differences visually assessed via spline shape and magnitude.

1.3 Results

Atmospheric temperature dynamics across the Neogene

Across the Neogene, mean atmospheric temperatures declined (Figure 2A). Overall, the cooling trend was shared across continents in northern latitudes. North America experienced greater variation in Ŷ, dY and instability. In contrast, Europe experienced relative warmer and more stable atmospheric temperature means across the Neogene. For instance, while mean temperature decreased from ~10.3 °C to ~4.5 °C across all northern latitudes in the 20Ma interval. In North America, temperatures declined from ~9.2 °C to ~2.8 °C, whereas Europe from ~13.3 °C to ~10.6 °C (Figure 2A). Similarly, the rate of cooling was consistently stronger in North America than in Europe throughout the Neogene. In North America, mean cooling rates intensified from approximately −0.17 °C Myr⁻¹ at ~22 Ma to a pronounced minimum of about −0.5 °C Myr⁻¹ during the late Miocene (~8–7 Ma), before weakening rapidly toward the late Neogene (2 Ma). (Figure 2B). This interval of accelerated cooling coincided with elevated climatic instability, which increased through the middle to late Miocene (~7-2 Ma) (Figure 2C).  In Europe, there is a steady decrease in the rate of cooling from (-0.3 °C Myr⁻¹ at ~22 Ma to ~0 °C Myr⁻¹ at ~2 Ma (Figure 2B). European temperature instability was consistently lower than in North America, reaching a minimum during the middle Miocene (Figure 2C).

Richness of fossil assemblages across the Neogene

After filtering and aggregation, the Neogene fossil dataset comprised 1,040 mammal species (427 genera) distributed across 1,407 fossil localities in Europe, and 731 species (286 genera) across 476 localities in North America. In both continents, herbivores represented the largest component of the fauna, accounting for 644 species and 253 genera in Europe, and 451 species and 170 genera in North America. Carnivores were less diverse, with 360 species (155 genera) in Europe and 280 species (116 genera) in North America, while omnivorous taxa (primarily primates) were rare and restricted only to Europe (36 species, 19 genera).. Sampling density differed markedly between continents: North America exhibited a higher mean number of genera per locality than Europe, a pattern that was consistent across trophic guilds. (Table S1)

Genus level accumulation curves show that overall, fossil assemblages in North America show lower standardized fossil genus richness when compared to Europe. Herbivores generally exhibit the steepest accumulation and highest asymptotic richness within most time slices, whereas carnivores accumulate more gradually and tend to saturate at lower richness values (Figure S8).

Fossil assemblage dissimilarity varies in space and time

Geographic and temporal distances had the strongest relative influence in the explained variance of fossil assemblages. In North America, space contributed 18% and time 68% while in Europe space contributed 70% and time 12% (Figure 3).  In North America, temporal distances are the strongest determinant of fossil turnover for herbivores (space = 6%, time = 66%) and for carnivores (space = 1%, time = 92%). In Europe, geographic distances are the strongest determinant of fossil turnover for herbivores (space = 72%, time = 20%) and for carnivores (space = 43%, time = 37%) (Figure 3).  

Across all taxa, the mean compositional turnover was 0.60 ± 0.17.  Mean turnover was similar between Europe and North America (Europe: 0.60; North America: 0.61). By disaggregating turnover by trophic levels, we find that Herbivores showed slightly higher mean turnover in North America (0.56) than in Europe (0.53), while carnivores showed lower mean turnover overall, 0.52 in Europe and 0.48 in North America. (Table S2). 

Temporal turnover, measured as the partial ecological distance along the geological time axis (2–23 Ma), increased toward deeper time in both continents. In other words, fossil assemblages are increasingly dissimilar as pairwise comparisons span intervals from the early Neogene. In Europe, herbivores showed a progressive increase in fossil turnover towards deeper times, but carnivores showed steepest changes (Figure 4A). In North America, herbivores and showed pronounced increases across the time axis, whereas carnivores increased more moderately and tended to level off at older ages (Figure 4C).

Spatial turnover measured as partial ecological distance along the geographic distance axis showed clear continental differences. In Europe, ecological distance increased steeply with geographic distance for all mammals. More steeply for herbivores and more gradually for carnivores (Figure 4B). In North America, spatial turnover was weaker than in Europe. Herbivores showed only a modest increase in turnover with distance, and carnivores showed little predictable change in fossil assemblage composition with distance alone (Figure 4D).

Changes in the atmospheric temperature shape fossil assemblage dissimilarity

Climatic turnover, measured as partial ecological distance along gradients of climate change magnitude, mean temperature, and climatic instability, showed consistent but guild-specific responses across the Neogene. In general, turnover tended to be lower during strong cooling and higher during weaker cooling or warming, a pattern driven largely by herbivores.

Across all trophic guilds, climatic predictors explain less variation than space and time, with a 12% of the explained variation attributed to changes in dY (Magnitude of climate change), which emerged as the dominant climatic driver of fossil turnover (Figure 3). In North America, 28% of the explained variance among herbivore fossils was attributed to dY, while for carnivore fossils 3%. In Europe, 9% of the explained variance of herbivore fossils was attributed to dY, while for carnivore fossils was 20% (Figure 3).

Along the climate change magnitude axis, North America shows a strong herbivore response. Under strong cooling (≈ −1.5 °C Myr⁻¹), turnover is near zero, but it increases steeply toward weaker cooling and warming, reaching ~0.60–0.65 before leveling off (Figure 5A). Carnivores in North America respond only weakly, with turnover remaining below ~0.05 across the gradient. In Europe, the pattern is reversed, herbivores show a modest increase, rising to ~0.15–0.20 and then stabilizing, whereas carnivores respond strongly, increasing from near zero under strong cooling (≈ −0.8 °C Myr⁻¹) to ~0.50 under warming (Figure 5B). Only herbivores in North America, respond to climatic instability, with turnover rising at low instability but quickly plateauing, while carnivores remain largely flat (Figure 3C). In Europe, instability has little effect on either guild (Figure 3D). Along the mean temperature gradient, mean temperature influences North American carnivore turnover (Figure 5E) whereas Europe shows weak responses overall (Figure 5F).
